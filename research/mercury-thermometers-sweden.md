# Mercury Thermometers in Sweden – Phase-out Research Notes

Related to: Quicksilver / mercury element, temperature measurement history.

## Why removed from Swedish temperature measurements

- **Toxicity**: Mercury (Hg, quicksilver) is highly toxic. Vapor inhalation and environmental release cause severe health and ecological harm.
- **Bans timeline**:
  - 1991–1992: Product-specific ban on mercury-containing measuring instruments (thermometers, etc.). Manufacture and sale prohibited.
  - Virtually all mercury thermometers phased out from the Swedish market.
  - 2009 (1 June): General national ban on mercury and articles containing it. No placement on market, use, or commercial export (with limited exemptions).
- **Alternatives**: Digital thermometers, alcohol-based, Galinstan (Ga/In/Sn alloy), etc. became standard. Hospitals essentially mercury-free.
- **Campaigns**: Collection/exchange programs in the 1990s (e.g., free digital replacements via pharmacies) to recover existing mercury thermometers.
- **Impact**: ~95% reduction in mercury sold in products between 1992–2003.

## How mercury was used for temperature

Mercury expands uniformly with temperature. In a glass capillary with a bulb reservoir, the rising column provides a readable scale. Superior to alcohol for higher ranges and accuracy historically (Fahrenheit commercialized mercury thermometers ~1714–1717; Celsius scale defined using mercury-compatible fixed points).

## Calibration insight (human design)

The mercury does not “know” the temperature. It simply expands/contracts with heat according to physics (thermal expansion).

Humans designed the glass tube and etched the scale so that the height of the mercury column matches fixed reference points (e.g. ice point = 0 °C, boiling point = 100 °C). The numbers are arbitrary human conventions mapped onto the physical expansion. Entropy/physics drives the movement; calibration makes it readable as degrees.
